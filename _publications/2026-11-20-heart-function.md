Although almost universally accepted, the claim that the heart’s function is to pump blood is mistaken. If that were its function, any arbitrary rate of cardiac output would be performance of the function. Restricting the rate to a range merely compatible with life would also be inadequate, depriving the function attribution of a unifying principle. This leads to a neglected but necessary concept: biological virtue. Just as Aristotle described moral virtue as doing the right thing in the right way, biological virtue entails performing a function rightly—timed, located, and calibrated for organismic flourishing. This framework applies not only to the heart but to all biological functions. We respond to key objections: that virtue requires reflection, that reasons-responsiveness is misapplied, that iological normativity is illusory, and that function statements and specifications are conflated. We also challenge Justin Garson’s “proximal function” view. Ultimately, we propose that understanding functions through biological virtue offers a richer, more coherent explanatory model—and a spur to renewed empirical investigation in biology.
